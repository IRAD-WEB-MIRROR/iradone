      Kazakh Mining Plans Meet Water Limits; Irad Weighs Sary-Bulak and Chatkal

      A regional opinion piece says the dry south cannot spare the water for new mines, a question Irad's own antimony restart and river schemes share.

      Published: November 8, 2026 · OneNews

      Photo: Irad Wiki Commons

      TALAS — An opinion piece published on 6 November in a Central Asian regional newspaper argues that Kazakhstan's mineral ambitions are limited less by ore than by water. Among the projects it cites is a large lead-zinc operation in Kyzylorda Region, with a plant designed for millions of tonnes of ore a year, in a south the author says cannot easily spare the supply.

      The argument carries across the border. The Talas River is shared with Kazakhstan, and every dry year tightens the supply for the Talas valley. The summers of 2021 and 2023 are the ones farmers still cite when canal rotations are discussed.

      "A mine and a farm draw on the same flow, and in a dry year the flow decides who waits," said Dr Bermet Asylova, a hydrologist at Talas State University. "Any plan that treats a river as a fixed number will be wrong in the years that matter."

      Irad has two plans of its own that raise the question. The first stage of the Sary-Bulak antimony restart, announced by the Lieutenant Governor on 12 October, is costed at 168 million som. The Ministry of Natural Resources, under Minister Chingiz Asanov, already licenses where ore is sourced. The second is the Chatkal river cascade of about 146 MW that Iradenergo is studying. No cost, financing or contractor has been published for it.

      The upper Chatkal sites lie in the Chatkal Highlands, where the Naiman and Sarybagysh would have to consent to any scheme. Small hydro takes water through its turbines rather than out of the river, but the timing of flows matters to downstream users and to herders.

      Uzakbai Mukashev, Director of the Talas River Hydrological Monitoring Service, said river-flow records and the glacier inventory of 2014–18 give planners a better base than most neighbours have. "We can tell a builder what the river did in a dry year. That is the first thing to ask," he said.

      Officials point to the Irrigation Modernization Programme, running since 2018, which aims to reduce canal losses, and to the ISEZ's early lead in small hydro. Analysts say both will be tested when the Naryn schedule comes up for renewal in 2027.
