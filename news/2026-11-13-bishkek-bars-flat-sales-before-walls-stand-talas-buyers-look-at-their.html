      Bishkek Bars Flat Sales Before Walls Stand; Talas Buyers Look at Their Own Sites

      Kyrgyzstan has ended off-plan sales at the foundation stage after more than 40 firms left buyers without flats, and nothing changes in Irad.

      Published: November 13, 2026 · OneNews

      Photo: IradOne

      TALAS — Kyrgyzstan has barred construction companies from selling flats while a building is still at the excavation or foundation stage. Under the new rule, units may be sold only once the structure of the building stands. Bishkek gave as its reason the cases of more than 40 construction firms that left buyers without the flats they had paid for.

      The change is a Kyrgyz one and does not apply in the Irad Special Economic Zone. Talas readers will still recognise the worry behind it. A flat is the largest purchase most families make, and in this city many of them pay for it with a mortgage, with savings, or with money sent home by relatives in the United States and Russia.

      The nearest local comparison is the Alma-Bak complex in western Talas, advertised on a billboard at the Trans-Talas Highway. Talas Turak-Jay Kurulush LLC is building 495 flats there, in eleven nine-storey blocks, on city land. The company's schedule has the complex due in 2027, and the work is on that schedule. Prices and lenders have not been published.

      Prof. Raisa Akmatova, an economist at Talas State University, said the Kyrgyz step shows what a buyer takes on before a wall exists. "Whoever pays at the excavation stage is in effect lending to the builder," she said. "In Talas the simple test is the one buyers already use: look at what is standing, ask who is lending, and keep every receipt." She added that dollar-based pricing adds a second risk, because the som has weakened on the bazaar since spring.

      Rents show the same dollar habit. A one-room flat rents for 1,000 to 1,500 som a month in Talas, and landlords in the newer towers often quote in dollars. Many families therefore see buying as the way out of the rent cycle.

      The other way Talas families build is slower and visible. On the Etek plots, allotted by the city from 2009, many large two-storey brick houses are still unfinished. Relatives abroad pay for them, and families add a floor or a wall as the money arrives. Professor Akmatova noted that this is a family building on its own plot with its own money, a different matter from buying a flat in a block before it is built. The plots in Etek and neighbouring Ak-Bosogo keep filling, and nothing at home has changed.

      Bishkek's rule takes effect in Kyrgyzstan only. Talas builders and buyers are watching how it works, and the city's own sites keep to their schedules.
