      Bishkek Agrees Second Waste-to-Energy Plant; Nurzhan's Air Reads 196

      As Kyrgyzstan settles on a second rubbish-burning power plant, Nurzhan's own winter smog raises the question of what the city's air is made of.

      Published: November 19, 2026 · OneNews

      Photo: kishjar? from Moscow, Russia / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0

      NURZHAN — The chairman of Kyrgyzstan's Cabinet of Ministers told parliament on 18 November that Bishkek will get a second waste-to-energy plant, and that the project has now been agreed. No such plant is proposed for the Irad Special Economic Zone. But the news arrived on a morning when Nurzhan's air was the worst of the three Iradi cities, and it has sent residents asking what that air is made of.

      Irad Gidromet's monitors read an AQI of 196 in Nurzhan today, rated unhealthy, with fine particles (PM2.5) at 119.0 micrograms per cubic metre. Iyık Janyl stood at 141 and Talas at 106, both rated unhealthy for sensitive groups. Nurzhan is under a temperature inversion, with a clear sky, a high of 2°C and a low of -5°C. District heating is running, as it does through the heating season.

      Mirlan Beishembiev, Director of Irad Gidromet, said the inversion is the immediate cause. "Cold air sits on the Chu plain like a lid, and whatever is released at the bottom stays at the bottom," he said. "The monitors count particles. They cannot tell us by themselves what was burned." He said readings usually ease within a day or two of the lid lifting.

      On sources, the figure most often cited is a 2021 study by Talas State University, which put household coal and district heating at about 45 per cent of Nurzhan's particulate pollution, the refinery and industry at about 25 per cent, traffic at about 20 per cent and dust at about 10 per cent. Nurzhan's central heat and power plant stands in the Eastern Industrial Zone and is owned by NurGaz. Its conversion to gas is planned but not yet funded. Smoke from private houses in belts such as Dastgoh District and Jangy-Konush adds to the winter load.

      Prof. Sergei Volkov, a petroleum engineer at Nurzhan Polytechnic Institute, said a gas conversion would cut the plant's share but could not clear the air alone. "The plant is one chimney. The coal stoves of a whole district are thousands of them," he said.

      Mayor Vadim Shcherbakov said refuse collection and heating arrangements in the city continue as they are. He said the administration expects the air to improve once the inversion breaks, and asked children, older residents and people with heart or lung conditions to stay indoors where possible and keep windows closed in the mornings. Irad Gidromet publishes readings for all three cities daily on gidromet.iradgov.org.
